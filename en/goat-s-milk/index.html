🥛 Goat's milk

Type of milk

Also known as: goat milk

Small fat globules and a different casein profile make it easier for many people to digest.

Goat’s milk is close to cow’s milk in its basic composition, with comparable amounts of protein, fat and lactose. According to the FAO, in 2010 it accounted for about 2.4% of world milk production; the largest producers are India, Bangladesh and Sudan. For rural people in many dry regions the goat is the main source of milk, and in the Mediterranean goat cheeses and yogurts are a notable part of the economy.

The differences lie in the details. Its fat globules are smaller on average, and its fat is richer in short- and medium-chain fatty acids: caproic, caprylic and capric acids are even named after the Latin word capra, “goat”. These and branched-chain fatty acids are linked to the typical “goaty” flavor. Goat’s milk has roughly ten times less vitamin B12 than cow’s milk, and both are low in folate.

Goat’s milk is often considered easier to digest, but the scientific evidence is mixed. Importantly, it contains the same major proteins, including beta-lactoglobulin, so according to the World Allergy Organization it is not a suitable substitute in cow’s milk protein allergy: cross-reactions are possible.

Frequently asked questions

How is goat’s milk different from cow’s milk?

They are similar in protein, fat and lactose. Goat’s milk has smaller fat globules, more short- and medium-chain fatty acids (hence its distinctive taste) and much less vitamin B12.

Can a child with cow’s milk allergy drink goat’s milk?

No: goat milk proteins are similar to cow milk proteins, and in IgE-mediated allergy severe cross-reactions are possible. The World Allergy Organization does not recommend this substitution.

Does goat’s milk contain lactose?

Yes, about as much as cow’s milk (around 4.5%), so it does not help with lactose intolerance.
