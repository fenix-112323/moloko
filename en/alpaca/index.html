🦙 Alpaca

Animal

Also known as: Vicugna pacos

A relative of the llama, prized for its wool.

The alpaca is a relative of the llama, raised in the Andes of Peru, Bolivia and Chile for its fine, soft fleece. Alpacas are not used for dairy: the milk is needed by their young.

Scientists have studied alpaca milk to develop substitutes for orphaned young. In the first days after birth the colostrum is very rich in protein; later the fat and lactose content rise. According to studies, mature alpaca milk contains about 4% fat and 5–6% protein, and its composition depends on pasture quality.

There are reports of an experimental semi-hard cheese made from alpaca milk, but it is more a scientific experiment than a tradition. In the Andean communities that raise alpacas, the dairy animals are cows, sheep and goats brought to South America from Europe.

Frequently asked questions

Are alpacas milked?

No, alpacas are raised for their fleece; their milk is studied only for feeding the young.

What is the difference between an alpaca and a llama?

The alpaca is smaller with finer, softer fleece; the llama is larger and used as a pack animal.

What is alpaca milk made of?

About 4% fat and 5–6% protein, although figures vary between studies.
