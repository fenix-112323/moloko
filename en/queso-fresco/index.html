🧀 Queso fresco

Dairy product 🏺 Traditional product

Also known as: Mexican fresh cheese

A soft, white, unripened Mexican cheese: milk is curdled with rennet or acid and drained. Spoils quickly.

Queso fresco (“fresh cheese”) is a soft, white, unripened cheese common in Mexico and other Latin American countries. It is made from cow’s milk (sometimes with some goat’s milk added): the milk is warmed and curdled — in some recipes with acid (lemon juice, vinegar) while heated almost to boiling, in others with rennet; the curd is drained, lightly salted and pressed into small rounds. The cheese is ready at once, with no ripening.

Queso fresco is moist, slightly salty, crumbly and milky in flavour. Because of its high moisture it spoils quickly and must be kept in the fridge. A close relative is queso blanco; in different regions these names are used for similar fresh white cheeses, and the line between them is blurred.

Queso fresco differs from bryndza and feta in not being aged in brine and in its milder taste. When heated it softens but hardly melts, so it is crumbled over tacos, enchiladas, beans and salads. Cheeses of this kind made from unpasteurised milk are linked to a risk of listeriosis, so pregnant women and children are advised to choose cheese made from pasteurised milk.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between queso fresco and feta?

Feta is an aged brined cheese made from sheep’s milk, while queso fresco is a fresh, unripened cheese, usually made from cow’s milk, softer and less salty.

Does queso fresco melt?

It softens when heated but hardly runs, so it is usually crumbled over finished dishes.

How long does queso fresco last?

It is a moist fresh cheese: usually only a few days or weeks in the fridge.
