🥛 Pasteurized milk

Type of milk

Also known as: pasteurised milk

Heated to 72–76 °C for 15–20 seconds: keeps for several days in the fridge.

Pasteurised milk is milk heated to a temperature below boiling to destroy disease-causing microorganisms. As defined by the FAO and WHO, pasteurisation must reliably kill the agents of tuberculosis (Mycobacterium tuberculosis) and Q fever (Coxiella burnetii). The minimum conditions are the equivalent of heating every particle of milk to 72 °C for 15 seconds in continuous flow, or to 63 °C for 30 minutes in a vat (batch pasteurisation).

The method is named after Louis Pasteur, who in the 1860s showed that briefly heating wine to 50–60 °C kills microbes without spoiling the taste. Pasteurisation of milk was proposed by Franz von Soxhlet in 1886, and from the early 20th century it sharply reduced the number of milk-borne diseases.

Pasteurisation is not sterilisation: it does not destroy bacterial spores, so such milk is kept in the fridge, usually for a few days to 2–3 weeks depending on the process and packaging. According to the FDA, pasteurisation has little effect on protein, fat, calcium and most vitamins; vitamin C suffers most, but milk is not a significant source of it anyway.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between pasteurised and raw milk?

Pasteurised milk has been heated to kill disease-causing bacteria, so it is safer. According to the FDA, the differences in nutritional value are minimal.

Do you need to boil pasteurised milk?

Usually not: it has already been treated. Boiling makes sense if the pack was opened long ago or the milk was kept out of the fridge.

How long does pasteurised milk last?

Unopened in the fridge, usually from a few days to 2–3 weeks; the exact date is on the pack.
