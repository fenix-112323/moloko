🥛 Cow's milk

Type of milk

Also known as: cow milk

The most widespread milk in the world: versatile, inexpensive and the basis of almost all dairy products.

Cow’s milk is the most widespread milk in the world and the main raw material of the dairy industry. According to the FAO, it accounted for about 83% of world milk production in 2010, and about 81% in the organisation’s later estimates. In statistics, “cows” include both humpless European cattle (Bos taurus) and humped zebu (Bos indicus): there is no reproductive barrier between them.

100 g of whole cow’s milk contains on average about 3.3 g of protein, 3.5–4 g of fat and about 4.7 g of lactose. About 80% of the protein is casein; the rest is whey proteins, including β-lactoglobulin, one of the main allergens in cow’s milk. Milk is a significant source of calcium, vitamin B12 and riboflavin.

About a third of the world’s dairy cows, according to the FAO, are Holsteins (Holstein-Friesians), valued for their high yields. Almost all familiar products are made from cow’s milk: drinking milk, cream and butter, kefir and yogurt, curd cheese, thousands of cheeses, condensed milk and milk powder. Because production is concentrated on cows, cow’s milk serves as the reference point when comparing the milk of other animals.

Frequently asked questions

How much fat is in cow’s milk?

Raw milk usually has 3–4%, sometimes up to 5.5% — depending on breed, feed and season. At the dairy, drinking milk is standardised to the desired fat level, for example 1.5%, 2.5% or 3.2%.

What share of the world’s milk comes from cows?

According to the FAO, cattle provide about 81–83% of world milk production; buffaloes come second.

Why are some people allergic to cow’s milk?

The immune system reacts to milk proteins — caseins or β-lactoglobulin. This is not the same as lactose intolerance, which is related to the sugar, not the protein.
