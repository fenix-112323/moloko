⚙️ Homogenizer

Equipment

Also known as: homogeniser

Breaks up fat globules under pressure so the cream doesn't rise.

A homogenizer is a machine that breaks up the fat globules in milk so the cream does not rise to the top. In essence, it is a powerful piston pump. It squeezes milk to 100–250 bar — 100–250 times normal air pressure — and forces it through a narrow gap about 0.1 mm wide. In the gap, the milk speeds up to hundreds of metres per second. The swirls at the exit tear the fat globules into tiny pieces.

The average globule size drops from about 3.5 micrometres to less than 1 micrometre. The milk must be hot for this, 55–80 °C: cold fat is partly solid and does not break up well. Many machines have two stages. The first breaks up the fat, and the second creates a little back pressure and separates globules that have clumped together. Almost all of the machine's energy turns into heat; less than 1% goes into the breaking up itself.

The homogenizer was invented by the Frenchman Auguste Gaulin. According to the Tetra Pak handbook, this happened in 1899, and the machine was shown to the public at the 1900 World's Fair in Paris. Today homogenizers stand in almost every dairy: on the lines for drinking milk, cream, yogurt and ice cream.

Frequently asked questions

How does a milk homogenizer work?

A piston pump forces hot milk through a narrow gap under high pressure. Strong swirls form at the exit, and they break the fat globules down to less than a micrometre.

What pressure is milk homogenized at?

Usually 10–25 MPa, that is 100–250 bar. For ice cream and some other products, the pressure can be different.
