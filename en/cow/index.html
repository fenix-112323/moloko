🐄 Cow

Animal

Also known as: dairy cow, cattle, Bos taurus

The world's main source of milk: ≈83% of global milk output (FAO, 2010). Dairy breeds give 20–40 L a day.

The cow is the planet’s main dairy species: according to the FAO, cow’s milk accounts for about four-fifths of world milk output. A cow starts giving milk after calving; a standard lactation is considered to be about 300 days, followed by a dry period before the next calving. In specialised breeds such as the Holstein, kept in more than 150 countries, daily yield at peak lactation can reach 20–40 litres or more.

Yields, however, depend heavily on conditions. In hot climates and on pasture-based farms people keep local cattle and zebu — they give less milk but cope better with heat, disease and poor fodder. That is why many countries cross local cows with dairy breeds to combine hardiness and yield.

The largest producers of cow’s milk are India, the United States and China. It is made into almost the whole range of dairy products, from butter and curd cheese to hard cheeses.

Frequently asked questions

How much milk does a cow give per day?

It depends on breed and feeding: high-yielding dairy cows at peak lactation give 20–40 litres or more, while local cattle in the tropics and on pasture give several times less.

Why do cows produce milk?

As in all mammals, milk is produced after the birth of young. For lactation to resume, a cow has to calve regularly — usually once a year.

What share of the world’s milk comes from cows?

About 80%, according to FAO estimates; the rest comes from buffalo, goats, sheep, camels and other species.
