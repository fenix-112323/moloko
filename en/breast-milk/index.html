🥛 Breast milk

Type of milk

Also known as: human milk, mother's milk

The ideal food for a baby: antibodies, lactoferrin, oligosaccharides; its composition changes as the child grows.

Breast (human) milk is the natural food of infants. WHO and UNICEF recommend exclusive breastfeeding for the first 6 months of life — no other food or drink, not even water — followed by complementary foods while breastfeeding continues up to two years and beyond. The first milk after birth, colostrum, is especially rich in antibodies and immune substances.

Human milk differs markedly from cow’s milk in composition. It has about a third as much protein (around 1 g per 100 g) but more lactose — about 7% — so the two are comparable in calories. Caseins make up less than 40% of the protein (about 80% in cow’s milk), there is no β-lactoglobulin, and in the stomach it forms a soft, easily digested curd. An important component of human milk is oligosaccharides (about 1.3 g per 100 g), which are thought to support the growth of beneficial bifidobacteria in the baby.

The composition of breast milk is not constant: it changes from colostrum to mature milk, during a single feed and depending on the mother’s diet (for example, in water-soluble vitamins). For babies who cannot receive breast milk, industry produces infant formula, usually based on cow’s milk; donor milk banks also exist.

Frequently asked questions

How many months should you breastfeed?

WHO recommends exclusive breastfeeding for the first 6 months, then continuing to breastfeed alongside complementary foods up to two years and beyond.

How is breast milk different from cow’s milk?

It has less protein and fewer minerals, more lactose and oligosaccharides, a different protein profile and many immune components. That is why whole cow’s milk is not suitable as an infant’s main food.

What is colostrum?

The first milk produced in the first days after birth. It is especially rich in antibodies and protective substances.
